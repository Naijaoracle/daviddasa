Africa's Digital Public Health Future: Five Priorities for Action

March 2, 2026 Editorial
Digital Health Africa CDC Health Systems DHIS2 Health Informatics Public Health

TLDR

Africa CDC's leadership outlines five concrete priorities for digitising Africa's public health infrastructure: universal digital health identifiers, smartphone-equipped community health workers on DHIS2, a second-generation integrated DHIS2 platform, a continental data centre, and an AI-powered knowledge management hub. The authors frame digitisation not as a technological challenge but as a political and financial imperative, and call for a self-sustaining financing model that frees the continent from dependence on donor-driven pilot cycles.

From Pilots to Scale

Africa is projected to have a population of 1.7 billion by 2030. That demographic trajectory is both a burden and an opportunity: a vast, young, mobile population that strains fragmented paper-based health systems, but also one that could benefit enormously from a coherent digital infrastructure if the continent moves decisively and soon.

This editorial from the leadership of the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC) arrives at a moment when the gap between ambition and reality in African digital health is hard to ignore. There is no shortage of pilots, platforms, and proof-of-concept tools. What has been missing is a clear, actionable continental agenda that connects those efforts into something that can actually scale. The authors offer exactly that: five priorities, grounded in existing infrastructure and realistic about what is required to move from fragmented beginnings to genuine health system transformation.

Writing in the Journal of Public Health in Africa, Ngongo, Dereje, Hall, and Kaseya situate these priorities within Africa's broader Health Security and Sovereignty (AHSS) agenda: the ambition for African nations to finance, manufacture, and govern their own health systems and medical countermeasures. Digital transformation is one of AHSS's strategic pillars. These five priorities are its operational specification.

The Five Priorities

1. A Universal Digital Health Identifier

Every African Union member state should issue a digital health card with a unique identifier at birth, one that follows the individual across the entire life course and across sectors. The authors envision an identifier that connects birth registration, immunisation records, school enrolment, and adult health and social records into a single portable continuum. That digital health wallet would evolve into a lifelong electronic medical record, functional across borders and particularly valuable for a population that is increasingly mobile.

The case for this is strong. Fragmented paper registries, lost vaccination cards, and weak civil registration systems are not abstract problems. They cost lives: zero-dose children fall through gaps, cross-border outbreak surveillance fails because people cannot be traced, and resource allocation is guesswork rather than evidence. A rights-based, interoperable individual identifier would address all of these simultaneously. Despite the relevance of this, the majority of African countries have not yet issued such identifiers, which makes this the foundational priority on which much else depends.

2. Digitising Community Health Workers and Primary Care

Community health workers (CHWs) are the frontline of Africa's health systems. Most of them still work with paper tools, which means critical surveillance data is slow, incomplete, and prone to transcription error. The authors propose equipping every CHW with a smartphone preloaded with the DHIS2 tracker platform, an open-source system already in use across all AU member states.

The same logic extends upstream to primary healthcare facilities. Health centres that currently depend on paper registers and delayed reporting should be provided with digital tools running DHIS2 tracker, enabling real-time monitoring of immunisation coverage, maternal health indicators, and supply chain data. The potential shift here is significant: reporting delays that currently take months could be reduced to minutes, and early outbreak warning systems could become genuinely functional rather than aspirational.

3. A Second-Generation Integrated DHIS2

DHIS2 is already Africa's most widely used health information system. The problem is that most deployments are programme-specific silos: HIV here, malaria there, maternal health somewhere else, emergency response managed separately again. The authors call for a second generation of DHIS2 that integrates routine programme monitoring with public health emergency management in a single platform.

This would allow Ministries of Health to track endemic diseases alongside acute outbreaks on one dashboard, with standardised indicators and interoperability with civil registration, laboratory, and supply chain systems. The framing is important: this is not about replacing DHIS2 but about maturing it. Africa's move from parallel systems to integrated, flexible digital architecture is the natural next step, and the infrastructure to support it already exists in the field.

4. A Continental Public Health Data Centre

Data sovereignty is a recurring theme in African digital health discourse, and with good reason. A continental data centre hosted at Africa CDC headquarters would provide a secure, centralised repository for Africa's public health data, with role-based access for member states. Beyond sovereignty, it would enable comparative analytics across countries, integrate with the Africa Pathogen Genomics Initiative and vaccine deployment platforms, and serve as the backbone for collective epidemic intelligence.

The authors frame this as a matter of continental health security. An Africa that can host, manage, and analyse its own health data is an Africa that is not dependent on external infrastructure for the most critical functions of epidemic preparedness and response.

5. A Continental Knowledge Management Hub

The fifth priority is perhaps the most forward-looking: a real-time health knowledge exchange hub, powered by AI, that can identify and archive health knowledge generated from member state data, synthesise and summarise it in accessible forms, and disseminate it to public health practitioners across the continent. The vision is a one-stop resource ensuring that health knowledge is not just available but usable, particularly during public health emergencies when the volume of relevant information can overwhelm decision-makers.

Natural language processing, automated evidence synthesis, and AI-driven anomaly detection are the technologies that would animate this hub. Crucially, the authors acknowledge that this requires parallel investment in digital literacy, ethical frameworks, and data governance to ensure AI-driven solutions remain equitable and rights-based.

The Enablers: Connectivity, AI, and Interoperability

None of the five priorities can function without reliable connectivity at the point of care. The authors are direct about this: without stable internet access and electricity at primary healthcare facilities, digital innovations cannot operate effectively or equitably. Many rural clinics remain offline. Addressing this requires public-private partnerships with telecommunications operators, scaled satellite and mobile broadband solutions, and systematic integration of connectivity requirements into health infrastructure investments so that every new or rehabilitated clinic is digital-ready from the start.

Interoperability standards get specific mention: HL7-FHIR, the International Patient Summary, and Integrating the Healthcare Enterprise profiles are identified as the globally recognised frameworks that should anchor national digital health strategies. These are not optional technical choices. Without them, Africa risks replicating at scale the same fragmentation that already characterises its current landscape, only now in digital form.

On AI, the authors are both enthusiastic and measured. AI-driven analytics can detect anomalies in surveillance data, predict epidemic hotspots, optimise resource allocation, and support clinical decision-making at the frontline. Machine learning and natural language processing, including voice-to-text reporting for CHWs in remote areas, are explicitly named. But the authors also note that realising these benefits requires investment in digital literacy, ethics, and governance. The technology is available; the ecosystem to deploy it safely is still being built.

Why This Matters

The most clarifying sentence in this editorial is also its central argument: "The digitisation of Africa's public health is no longer a technological challenge; it is a political and financial imperative." That reframing matters. Framing digitisation as a technology problem locates the obstacle in the wrong place and produces the wrong solutions, more platforms, more tools, more pilots that never scale. Framing it as a political and financial problem puts the obstacle where it actually sits: in the absence of sustained commitment and the continued dependence on donor-driven funding cycles that cannot support long-term systems maintenance.

The authors' call for a self-sustaining business model, blending domestic investment, public-private partnerships, and regional innovation funds, reflects hard-won experience with the limits of external dependency. Health systems built on donor enthusiasm tend to survive only as long as that enthusiasm persists. Africa needs digital health infrastructure that outlasts any single funding cycle.

For those working in digital health on the continent, this editorial reads as a mandate. The five priorities are not aspirational visions for 2050; they are feasible next steps that build on existing infrastructure (DHIS2 is already everywhere), existing institutions (Africa CDC already exists), and existing policy frameworks (AHSS is already the continental agenda). What they require is the political will to move from fragmented pilots to coordinated, sustained implementation at scale.

There is also an implicit message here for the global AI and digital health community: Africa is not waiting to be the recipient of tools designed elsewhere. The knowledge management hub, AI-powered and grounded in Africa-generated data, is a vision of the continent as a producer of health intelligence, not just a consumer of it. That ambition is worth taking seriously.
