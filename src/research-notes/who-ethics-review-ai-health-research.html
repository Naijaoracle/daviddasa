WHO Ethics Review and Oversight of AI-Related Health Research

August 5, 2026 Report
WHO Research Ethics AI Governance Health Research RECs

TLDR

The WHO's Artificial intelligence-related health research: ethics review and oversight examines how established research ethics principles and review mechanisms should adapt to the growing use of AI in health research. The report distinguishes between research that analyses health data using AI, research conducted with AI tools, and research evaluating AI-based health technologies. It argues that existing oversight arrangements may fail because AI research can move rapidly into deployed products, generate harms without direct interaction with participants, blur the distinction between health and personal data, and be conducted outside traditional biomedical research institutions.

The report does not propose expanding research ethics committees into universal AI regulators. Instead, it advances a distributed governance model in which ethical responsibility extends across the research lifecycle. RECs would undertake risk-proportionate review, require stronger information from researchers and remain engaged where risks evolve. Researchers would apply ethics by design, disclose model limitations and internal testing, protect crowd workers, assess wider social impacts and build equitable international partnerships. Funders, data custodians, journals, regulators and scientific societies would provide complementary layers of oversight.

Why Existing Research Ethics Oversight Is Not Fit for Purpose

Research ethics governance has traditionally been organised around clearly defined research protocols, identifiable investigators and prospective interactions with human participants. AI-related health research frequently does not conform to this model.

The report identifies six structural weaknesses in current arrangements. The legal boundaries determining which activities require ethics review are often vague or exclude newer forms of AI research. Existing ethics guidance does not fully address AI-specific concerns. REC operating models may not correspond to when, where or how AI research is conducted. Committees may lack adequate technical expertise and institutional support. Researchers may not integrate ethics into study design. Other organisations involved in research quality and governance may also fail to manage AI-related risks.

These limitations are particularly important because the interval between research and deployment may be substantially shorter for AI technologies than for conventional medical products. A methodological or ethical problem left unresolved during research may therefore be incorporated quickly into a commercial product or health system. The report also notes that outputs developed for one population may be inappropriate or harmful when applied elsewhere.

AI research can also be performed without direct interaction with research participants. The use of anonymised, publicly available or previously collected data may lead researchers to conclude that ethics oversight is unnecessary, even where studies create privacy risks, discriminatory outcomes or group-level harms.

The accessibility of AI and data science tools introduces another governance problem. Individuals and companies without conventional health research training can conduct studies, develop health applications or analyse large data sets. Existing definitions of research may not clearly capture activities such as commercial product development, reinforcement learning involving crowd workers or local adaptation of large multimodal models.

Three Categories of AI-Related Health Research

WHO divides the field into three categories.

The first is health-related data science research using AI. This includes the application of AI to large data sets for biomedical, clinical, epidemiological, behavioural or social science research.

The second is research conducted with AI tools. AI may be used to formulate hypotheses, design studies, identify participants, monitor research activity or produce quantitative and qualitative outputs.

The third is research on AI tools and technologies. This includes studies evaluating the safety, utility, effectiveness or implementation of AI applications used for diagnosis, treatment, detection or behavioural influence.

These categories are analytically useful, but they may overlap in practice. A single project may use generative AI to develop its protocol, train a model on health data and evaluate the resulting system in a clinical environment. Governance should therefore examine individual activities and stages rather than relying solely on a single classification for the whole project.

Ethical Oversight Beyond Formal REC Review

One of the report's most consequential positions is that exemption from REC review should not be treated as exemption from ethical accountability.

Health data research using AI may raise concerns relating to informed consent, privacy, data governance, group harms, participant selection, the distribution of risks and benefits, and the treatment of crowd workers. These issues may require review by a data access committee, specialist body or another oversight mechanism even when conventional REC review is not legally required.

This distinction is particularly important for de-identified data. Data may still be vulnerable to re-identification, and research findings may affect communities or populations even where no individual is directly identifiable. Scientific validity and social value also remain ethical requirements. A study that produces unreliable conclusions can waste resources, distort subsequent research and contribute to harmful health decisions.

The report therefore resists a binary model in which research is either subject to full REC review or ethically unrestricted. It instead supports differentiated oversight according to the nature and severity of the risks involved.

A Distributed Lifecycle Governance Model

WHO's central institutional recommendation is that responsibility should be shared across the research lifecycle.

RECs remain indispensable, but they cannot serve as a catch-all mechanism for every AI research activity. Expanding their remit without additional capacity could overwhelm an already constrained system. Data access committees, scientific societies, health systems, data protection authorities and specialised review bodies may therefore need to assume more prominent roles.

This model also addresses the timing problem. Some ethical risks may not be visible when a committee first reviews a protocol. The model, training data, intended users or deployment setting may change later. Oversight may therefore need to continue through validation, implementation and post-research use rather than ending when initial approval is granted.

The report envisages coordinated but distinct responsibilities. Researchers and sponsors would identify and mitigate risks from the earliest design stages. Funders could make training, transparency and risk management conditions of funding. RECs would review activities falling within their remit. Data access committees would govern access to sensitive data. Journals could assess ethical conduct and reporting integrity. Regulators would examine technologies entering clinical use.

The practical weakness of this model is that the report does not establish a definitive routing system. Institutions must still decide which body leads, when another body must be consulted, how conflicting decisions are resolved and who is ultimately accountable for an oversight failure.

Strengthening Research Ethics Committees

The report recommends substantial investment in REC capacity. Institutions should provide financial, human and administrative resources, update standard operating procedures and offer continuing education in AI-related research methods and ethics. Committees should include members with relevant technical and ethical expertise, while networks of RECs should share experience at local, national and regional levels.

Countries with relatively limited review capacity could establish a specialised national or regional REC for AI-related health research. Such a body could concentrate expertise and promote consistency, although it would need to remain responsive to local clinical, legal and cultural contexts.

The report also calls for stronger scrutiny of private sector research. Commercial developers should disclose information needed for independent assessment, while governments should examine whether company-operated review processes exercise genuine independence. Public and private collaborations require safeguards to ensure that public-interest objectives are not displaced by commercial priorities.

For multi-site and multinational research, WHO supports greater cooperation, joint review and common ethical standards. Additional local approval may be appropriate where research presents more than minimal risk, will produce a technology intended for use in that country, or has consequences beyond the use of locally sourced data.

Information Researchers Should Provide

RECs cannot evaluate AI research effectively unless researchers disclose more than a conventional clinical protocol normally contains.

WHO suggests requiring an explicit risk classification and supporting rationale. Researchers may also need to submit equity and environmental impact assessments, internal evaluations, descriptions of training and validation data, and the results of bias testing. Environmental assessment could include the potential carbon and water footprint of an AI intervention if deployed at scale.

The report also supports affected-community participation. REC membership or consultation processes could include patient representatives, crowd workers or people with lived experience of the conditions and populations likely to be affected.

These requirements could provide the foundation for a standard AI research annex to ethics applications. The report, however, does not define minimum evidence thresholds, accepted testing methods or the conditions under which identified risks should prevent a study from proceeding.

Responsibilities of Researchers

The report rejects a compliance-only understanding of research ethics. Researchers should apply ethical principles from initial design through completion and consideration of long-term consequences.

Researchers should complete relevant training, involve health professionals early and apply ethics by design to safety, cybersecurity, privacy and bias risks. Before research involving human beings or commercial testing, they should undertake appropriate error testing using current methods.

The recommendations extend beyond model performance. Researchers should consider effects on health equity, environmental impacts and equitable access to resulting technologies. They should disclose negative or inconclusive internal evaluations, describe which AI tools were used and explain how those tools were applied, including relevant prompts used with generative AI systems.

The report also recognises crowd workers as an ethical concern. Researchers and institutions should examine the labour practices of intermediary companies and seek to ensure that workers receive appropriate protections regardless of where they are located or whether they formally qualify as research participants.

Equity and Low and Middle Income Countries

WHO places international inequality near the centre of its analysis. Much AI research and technology development is concentrated in North America, Europe and East Asia, while technologies may later be tested or deployed in settings that were not meaningfully represented during design. This can produce systems that are poorly adapted to local needs, expose communities to exploitative research practices and generate products that are inaccessible to the populations whose data supported their development.

The report addresses fairness, benefit sharing, health data colonialism, ethics dumping, power inequalities and capacity building. Researchers should collaborate with institutions in low and middle income countries in the co-creation of data sets, research designs and findings. Funders should provide resources that enable local researchers to lead research rather than merely supply data or participants.

Equity therefore concerns not only whether a model performs accurately across demographic groups, but also who determines the research agenda, who controls data, who receives funding and whether resulting technologies are affordable and accessible.

The Use of AI in Ethics Review

The report considers whether AI itself could assist RECs. Potential applications include checking that submissions are complete, identifying recurring ethical concerns, locating relevant precedents and providing a first-pass review of lengthy protocols.

Possible benefits include greater consistency across committees, faster review, reduced administrative burden and better identification of salient issues. WHO nevertheless frames these systems as adjuncts rather than substitutes for human judgement.

The risks are substantial. Generative models can hallucinate, overlook serious concerns and expose confidential material. Reviewers may defer excessively to model recommendations, particularly where committee members lack confidence or technical training. Models may also fail to account for local law, institutional practice, language or culture. Most fundamentally, ethics review depends on human deliberation and dialogue, which should not be replaced by automated analysis.

General-purpose language models have also shown uneven performance across different ethical questions. Domain-specific systems may perform better, but would require curation, testing, governance and resources that may not be equally available across countries. AI tools could additionally be used by applicants to tailor their wording to avoid automated red flags without resolving the underlying ethical problem.

From Principles to Implementation

The report provides a strong conceptual framework, but it is not yet a complete operational standard. It does not establish mandatory review thresholds, model performance criteria, reporting intervals, audit requirements or rules for resolving overlap between oversight bodies.

Its recommendations are best understood as the basis for national regulation and institutional implementation. A practical framework would need:

  1. A screening and risk-classification process.
  2. Defined referral routes between RECs and other oversight bodies.
  3. Standard documentation for data provenance, validation and model limitations.
  4. Equity, privacy, security and societal impact assessments.
  5. Mandatory review following substantial changes to models, data or intended use.
  6. Incident reporting and escalation procedures.
  7. Rules governing the use of generative AI by researchers and reviewers.
  8. Clear assignment of institutional accountability.

WHO itself calls for standard-setting organisations to update existing research ethics guidance more frequently and to develop toolkits containing templates, checklists, case examples and training materials.

Assessment

The report's most important contribution is its rejection of one-time REC approval as the complete answer to AI research ethics. Its distributed lifecycle model reflects the reality that risks can emerge during data collection, model development, validation, deployment and subsequent reuse.

Its principal limitation is the gap between governance principle and operational execution. Shared responsibility can strengthen oversight only where institutional roles, decision rights and accountability are clearly assigned. Without that clarity, the model may produce duplicated review in some areas and no effective oversight in others.

The report should therefore be treated as a foundation for detailed national guidance, institutional standard operating procedures and AI-specific ethics review tools. It establishes the direction of travel clearly: ethical governance of AI-related health research must be continuous, technically informed, socially responsive and broader than the jurisdiction of any single committee.
