HHS Artificial Intelligence Strategy: Five Pillars for Health and Human Services

March 16, 2026 Strategy
AI Strategy HHS US Federal Health Policy Digital Health

TLDR

The US Department of Health and Human Services Artificial Intelligence Strategy organises the department's AI agenda around five pillars: governance and risk management, data infrastructure for user needs, workforce development and burden reduction, health research and reproducibility, and care and public health delivery modernisation. With 271 active AI implementations across HHS in fiscal year 2024 and a department-wide deployment of generative AI tools already underway, this is a strategy for scaling and governing what is already happening, not a blueprint for a distant future. It uses the NIST AI Risk Management Framework as its governance backbone and frames the OneHHS approach as a means of breaking down the silos that have historically fragmented the department's digital capacity.

A Department Already Running on AI

HHS is not a single organisation in any meaningful operational sense. It encompasses the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the Food and Drug Administration, the National Institutes of Health, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, the Administration for Children and Families, the Indian Health Service, and more than a dozen other operating divisions. Each has its own data infrastructure, its own regulatory mandate, its own mission. Coordinating AI adoption across this landscape is a genuinely difficult governance challenge.

What makes the HHS AI Strategy notable is that it starts from a position of scale rather than aspiration. By the time the strategy was published, 271 AI implementations were already active across the department. ChatGPT had been deployed to all HHS employees. The FDA was actively reviewing AI-enabled medical devices. CMS was using AI for fraud detection and claims processing. NIH was integrating AI into its research grant review and data infrastructure. The strategy does not announce the dawn of AI at HHS; it attempts to bring coherent governance to a process that has already begun.

The document carries forewords from Deputy Secretary Jim O'Neill and Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., framing it within the administration's broader emphasis on deregulation, operational efficiency, and scientific transparency. That political framing shapes some of the language but does not substantially alter the strategy's technical content.

The Five Pillars

1. Governance and Risk Management for Public Trust

The first pillar establishes the framework through which HHS manages the risks of AI deployment. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is the chosen standard, providing a structured approach to identifying, measuring, and mitigating AI risks across the department's diverse contexts. Pillar one also encompasses the governance structures: designated AI leads within operating divisions, a department-level AI governance council, and processes for reviewing high-risk AI use cases before deployment. Public trust is treated not as an outcome to be achieved once at launch, but as an ongoing obligation requiring continued transparency, accountability, and responsiveness to concerns as they arise.

2. Design Infrastructure and Platforms for User Needs

The second pillar addresses what the strategy calls the OneHHS AI-integrated Commons: a shared data and computing infrastructure that allows different divisions to share data assets, analytical tools, and AI capabilities without duplicating investment or creating incompatible systems. Central to this pillar is a commitment to FAIR data principles, making data Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable. The strategy identifies data fragmentation as one of the primary barriers to effective AI across HHS and treats the infrastructure pillar as the foundation on which everything else depends. A department that cannot share data across its own divisions cannot realise the benefits of AI at scale, regardless of how sophisticated its models become.

3. Promote Workforce Development and Burden Reduction for Efficiency

The third pillar acknowledges that technology alone does not produce transformation. Deploying AI tools to a workforce that lacks the skills or confidence to use them effectively is a reliable route to wasted investment. This pillar encompasses training programmes for existing staff, hiring strategies oriented toward AI-literate talent, and a specific focus on using AI to reduce administrative burden: the documentation load, the data entry requirements, the repetitive reporting tasks that consume clinical and administrative capacity that could be directed toward higher-value work. The deployment of generative AI tools to all HHS employees is presented here as a workforce modernisation initiative, not merely a productivity intervention.

4. Foster Health Research and Reproducibility through Gold-Standard Science

NIH's role within HHS gives this pillar particular weight. AI is transforming biomedical research at every level: accelerating drug discovery, enabling analysis of genomic datasets at previously impossible scale, generating hypotheses, and automating aspects of literature synthesis and evidence review. But AI also introduces new reproducibility risks: models that perform impressively on one dataset and poorly on another, results that depend on undisclosed methodological choices, and findings that are difficult to verify because the analytical process is opaque. This pillar commits HHS to applying the same standards of scientific rigour to AI-enabled research that it applies to clinical trials: transparency about methods, independent validation, and openness to replication.

5. Enable Care and Public Health Delivery Modernisation for Better Outcomes

The fifth pillar is the most direct expression of what all the others are meant to serve. AI should produce better health outcomes: more accurate diagnoses, more effective treatments, more responsive public health surveillance, better resource allocation in Medicare and Medicaid, and improved access for underserved populations. This pillar explicitly addresses the equity dimension, acknowledging that AI-driven health system transformation could widen disparities if deployed without attention to differential performance across demographic groups. It also addresses the clinical interface directly: AI should reduce the burden on clinicians, support rather than override clinical judgment, and be implemented in ways that strengthen rather than erode the clinician-patient relationship.

What Is Distinctive About This Strategy

Several features distinguish the HHS strategy from equivalent documents in other jurisdictions. The most striking is its starting point. With 271 active AI implementations already running, HHS is not drafting a vision; it is documenting and governing a reality. That scale creates both opportunity and risk. The opportunity is that lessons from existing deployments can inform governance design, and that successes can be replicated. The risk is that systems developed without unified governance may have embedded assumptions, data practices, or performance characteristics that will be difficult to audit retrospectively.

The OneHHS framing is also distinctive. It reflects an understanding that the barriers to effective AI in a large federal department are primarily organisational rather than technical. Different divisions have historically operated with substantial autonomy, separate IT systems, and limited data sharing. Building shared infrastructure and shared governance is a harder problem than selecting AI tools. The strategy acknowledges this directly and positions the infrastructure and governance pillars as prerequisites for everything else.

The decision to use the NIST AI Risk Management Framework rather than develop a proprietary HHS framework is pragmatically sound. NIST's framework is already in wide use across both the federal government and the private sector, meaning that vendors, contractors, and partner organisations are likely to be familiar with it. Using a common standard also simplifies audit and oversight.

For those working in health AI beyond the United States, the HHS strategy is worth reading as a case study in governing AI adoption at institutional scale. The challenges it identifies, data fragmentation, workforce capability gaps, reproducibility risks, equity implications, are not specific to the US federal government. They are the challenges facing any large health system that is trying to move from isolated AI pilots to coordinated, sustained deployment.
